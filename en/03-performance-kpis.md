# Measuring performance: KPIs, reports, and objectives

In an office you measure by what you see. In remote you see nothing: what you measure is the management. But most of what gets measured is noise. This part is the shared instrument for both seats — founder and worker — to measure what matters, without duplication and without kidding yourself.

**The mother rule.** A number that moves and changes no decision isn’t a KPI: it’s noise with decimals.

## 1 · KPI, metric, objective: not the same thing

They get muddled daily, and that muddle is what fills dashboards with useless numbers. Four different things.

- **Metric:** anything you can count. There are infinitely many, and most don’t matter.

- **Objective:** the outcome you want, the why. Not a number — a direction. “Get paid sooner” is an objective.

- **KPI (key performance indicator):** the metric — few — tied to an objective that actually changes what you do. The key word is “key”: if there are twenty, they aren’t.

- **Target:** the number that says “good.” A KPI with no target can’t be read: 40 is neither good nor bad until you know the target was 30.

**The KPI test.** Before adding an indicator, ask: if this number gets worse, what will I do differently? If there’s no answer, it isn’t a KPI. Delete it.

## 2 · KPI typologies

Knowing what type each indicator is avoids the commonest mistake: measuring only results and finding out too late.

| **Axis**                        | **What it separates**                                        | **Example**                                                                             |
|---------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Leading vs lagging**          | The leading predicts; the lagging confirms.                  | Leading: sales meetings booked. Lagging: this month’s revenue.                          |
| **Input · output · outcome**    | Activity done · thing produced · real impact.                | Input: proposals sent. Output: contracts signed. Outcome: revenue retained at 6 months. |
| **Quantitative vs qualitative** | A number vs a structured judgment.                           | Quantitative: 4% churn. Qualitative: team health.                                       |
| **By function**                 | Sales, product, delivery, finance, people.                   | Each area watches its own, not the neighbor’s.                                          |
| **Counter-metric (guardrail)**  | Watches that improving one KPI doesn’t break something else. | Ship faster without spiking bugs.                                                       |

**The balance that matters.** A dashboard of only lagging indicators is a rear-view mirror: you find out when you can no longer act. For every result you measure, keep at least one leading indicator that predicts it.

Leading/lagging and dashboard balance: standard management literature (balanced scorecard). Counter-metrics: common product practice to prevent gaming.

## 3 · How to prioritize them and kill duplicates

### Few, or none

Max 3 to 5 KPIs per person or team. With twenty indicators nobody watches any: attention doesn’t scale. Prioritizing is, above all, deciding what you won’t watch.

### The North Star

**North Star metric.** The single number that best sums up whether you’re creating value. It doesn’t replace the rest, but it rules: when two KPIs conflict, the one that moves it wins.

### Kill duplicates

Two KPIs that always rise and fall together measure the same thing under two names. Keep one — the one closest to the decision — and retire the other. A dashboard with no duplicates reads in ten seconds; one with duplicates doesn’t get read.

### Beware vanity metrics

**Vanity metric.** The one that always goes up and feels good but changes no decision: followers, messages sent, hours online. You spot them because they never drop and never hurt.

How to prioritize: for each candidate, write the specific decision that would change if it moved. Anything with no decision behind it, out. What’s left is your KPIs.

## 4 · The founder’s KPIs

At company level, the founder watches a compact board — one North Star and four or five supporting KPIs across functions — not a thirty-light dashboard.

| **KPI**                             | **Type**              | **Decision it drives**                              |
|-------------------------------------|-----------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| **Monthly recurring revenue (MRR)** | Lagging · output      | Hire or hold; pace of investment.                   |
| **Customer churn**                  | Lagging · outcome     | Fix the product before acquiring more.              |
| **Weighted pipeline**               | Leading               | Predicts revenue two months out; tune sales effort. |
| **Gross margin**                    | Lagging · output      | Whether cost arbitrage holds (Part I, 9).           |
| **Runway (months of cash)**         | Lagging               | How long you last; when to raise or cut.            |
| **Team health (eNPS)**              | Qualitative · leading | Predicts attrition before people leave.             |

**The founder’s trap.** Measuring a lot so you can decide nothing. Watch thirty numbers and you don’t govern — you gaze. Pick the North Star and four supporters, and check the rest only when one of those four breaks.

MRR, churn, pipeline, margin: standard recurring-revenue nomenclature. Runway and cash: basic startup finance.

## 5 · The worker’s KPIs

At the individual level, your KPIs measure outcome, not activity (consistent with ROWE, Parts I and II). You pick two or three that ladder up to the team’s, and put them in writing so whoever evaluates you watches the same thing you do.

### From role to KPI

| **Role type**                  | **Example KPI — outcome, not activity**                     |
|--------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Sales**                      | Revenue closed and retained, not calls made.                |
| **Marketing / growth**         | Qualified leads that convert, not impressions.              |
| **Product / engineering**      | Shipped work that works, not lines of code.                 |
| **Support / customer success** | Account retention and resolution time, not tickets touched. |
| **Operations**                 | Processes that cut cost or error, not tasks closed.         |

**Your trap as a worker.** Measuring your activity to look busy: hours online, messages, tasks moved. That’s theater. Measure the result that would be missing if you weren’t here tomorrow.

## 6 · How they tie to company objectives

**OKR (Objective + Key Results).** A method for tying work to strategy: an Objective — qualitative, the direction — and its Key Results (2 to 4 numbers that say whether you’re hitting it). The objective inspires; the key results are measured.

### The cascade

Every individual KPI must trace up to a company objective. That’s line of sight: the worker sees how their number moves the team’s, and the team’s moves the company’s. If a KPI ladders up to nothing, it’s surplus — or an objective is missing.

| **Level**   | **Format**             | **Example**                                                                     |
|-------------|------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Company** | Objective + North Star | “Be profitable without depending on one client” · MRR and client concentration. |
| **Team**    | Key results            | “Cut churn from 6% to 4% this quarter.”                                         |
| **Person**  | Individual KPI         | “Retain 95% of my accounts.”                                                    |

**The question that orders everything.** For any KPI, whoever’s: which company objective does it ladder up to? If nobody can answer, either the KPI is surplus or the company never wrote its objective.

OKR: John Doerr, “Measure What Matters,” 2018. Management by output: Andy Grove, “High Output Management,” 1983. Vanity metric: Eric Ries, “The Lean Startup,” 2011.

## 7 · Daily and monthly reports

### The daily report

It’s for coordinating and unblocking in async, not for surveillance. Short, written, same time each day.

- What I moved toward my KPI yesterday — as an outcome, not a task list.

- What I’ll do today.

- What’s blocking me and who I need.

A daily report that lists tasks is a presence log in disguise. Tie every line to an outcome, or don’t write it.

### The monthly report

It’s for seeing the trend, deciding, and tying everything back to objectives. Fixed structure.

- Each KPI against its target, and the trend versus last month.

- What it means, in one line, not a paragraph.

- What I change next month because of what the number says.

The founder does it at company level (monthly business review); the worker at the level of their accounts, and as input to their 1:1.

### Cadence: each report at its own pace

| **Pace**      | **Who**            | **For what**                              |
|---------------|--------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| **Daily**     | Person → team      | Coordinate and unblock.                   |
| **Weekly**    | Team               | Adjust the week’s course.                 |
| **Monthly**   | Founder and person | Trend, decision, tie to objectives.       |
| **Quarterly** | Company            | Review objectives and OKRs; rethink KPIs. |

**The report nobody reads.** If a report changes no decision, stop writing it. A report is to act on, not to file.

## 8 · The mistakes that kill a metrics system

- Measuring too much: twenty KPIs is zero KPIs. Attention doesn’t scale.

- Measuring activity instead of outcome: you count motion, not value.

- Only lagging indicators: you find out when you can no longer act.

- KPIs with no counter-metric: people improve the number by breaking what you don’t measure.

- Reports nobody reads or turns into a decision.

- KPIs that ladder up to no company objective.
