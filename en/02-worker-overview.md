# The remote worker’s overview: contractor or EOR

Working remotely for a company in another country is one of the best career moves there is: you earn against one market and live against another. But the setup matters. Being hired as an independent contractor is not the same as being hired through an EOR: it changes how you get paid, which taxes you owe, what’s covered, and what you’re entitled to. Almost nobody explains this to you; here we do.

**The first thing to know about yourself.** Before you sign, know which setup you’re entering. Everything else — your invoice, your taxes, your sick leave, your severance — hangs off that one word: contractor or EOR.

## 1 · The two setups at a glance

|                                                   | **Independent contractor**           | **EOR**                                                         |
|---------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Your legal employer**                           | Nobody: you’re your own business.    | The EOR, in your country. The client company directs your work. |
| **How you get paid**                              | You invoice; you’re paid gross.      | You get a payslip with taxes already withheld.                  |
| **Taxes**                                         | You declare and pay them in full.    | The EOR withholds and remits for you.                           |
| **Benefits (sick pay, unemployment, paid leave)** | None by default: you build your own. | Whatever your country’s law requires.                           |
| **Flexibility**                                   | High: several clients, your hours.   | Lower: you’re an employee, with its rules.                      |
| **Stability and protection**                      | Low: ends on short notice.           | High: local employment protection.                              |

## 2 · Independent contractor: freedom and responsibility

**Independent contractor.** You provide a service to a company through a commercial contract, not an employment one. You’re not their employee: you’re a vendor. You invoice them, and in exchange for more freedom you carry what an employer would normally cover.

### What you sign

- **Services contract:** scope, price, deadlines, how it ends and with how much notice. Read the notice clause: it’s your safety net.

- **IP assignment:** hands the client what you create for them. Normal — but check it doesn’t also grab what you make on your own time.

- **NDA (confidentiality):** what you can’t share. Make sure it’s reasonable in time and scope.

### What nobody covers

Paid holidays, sick leave, unemployment, an employer pension: none of it comes included. No invoice, no income. That’s why your contractor rate should be higher than an equivalent salary: it has to pay for all of that out of your own pocket.

**The buffer rule.** From day one, set aside a slice of every invoice for taxes and for the lean months. Getting paid gross fools you: part of it isn’t yours — it belongs to the tax office and to your future self in the month no work comes in.

## 3 · EOR: you’re an employee, even if your boss is eight zones away

With an EOR, a local company in your country is your official employer. You sign with them, get paid by them, and they give you what your country’s law requires. The company you actually work for — the client — directs your day-to-day, but legally isn’t your employer.

### What you gain

- A real employment contract, with your country’s protection.

- Taxes and contributions handled: you don’t file them yourself.

- Statutory benefits: paid leave, sick leave, and whatever your local law sets.

- Stability: you’re not cut on a contractor’s short notice.

### What you give up

Less flexibility: you’re an employee, with its hours and rules, not a vendor with several clients. And an extra layer between you and the client company for anything payroll.

**What to check in an EOR offer.** Who the EOR provider is, which local benefits your contract includes, and in what currency and how often you’re paid. The “salary” they offer may or may not include the EOR fee: ask.

## 4 · What suits you and what to negotiate

You don’t always get to choose: many companies already decide on their side for legal reasons (see Part I, section 7). But where you can influence it, decide with your head, not with the headline rate alone.

- **Contractor suits you if** you want several clients, a higher hourly rate, and to run your own business — and you can manage your taxes and your lean months.

- **EOR suits you if** you want stability, employment protection, and no wrestling with tax filing: you’d rather have a payslip and sleep easy.

And watch what you look like: if you work exclusively, on set hours, fully embedded in the team, and they still hire you “as a contractor,” you’re in misclassification territory. That exposes you and the company (see Part I, section 8).

## 5 · Getting paid: invoice, currency, and not losing on FX

### If you’re a contractor: the invoice

- **Net terms:** “net 30” means you’re paid up to 30 days after the invoice. Negotiate it: net 15 beats net 30 for your cash flow.

- **Currency and FX:** if you’re paid in dollars and spend in another currency, a traditional bank eats you on the exchange. Use a multi-currency account so you don’t bleed on every payment.

- **Platform:** many companies pay via Deel or similar; it puts contract, invoice, and payment in one place.

### If you’re on an EOR: the payslip

You’re paid net, with taxes already withheld, like any employee. Your job here is to understand what’s deducted and to check the currency and pay date match what you agreed.

Stack platforms: Deel (global hiring and pay). Common multi-currency accounts: Revolut, Wise, and equivalents. Compare fees before choosing.

## 6 · Nomad taxes: where you’re resident changes everything

If you work remotely and, on top of that, you move around, your tax bill stops being obvious. The question that governs it all isn’t “what country is my company from?” — it’s “where am I a tax resident?”

**Tax residency.** The country with the right to tax your worldwide income. It’s not your nationality or where your client is: it depends on where you live and how long you spend there.

**The 183-day rule.** In many countries, spend 183 days or more in a year and you’re treated as a tax resident there, owing tax on your worldwide income. It’s a guide, not a universal law: each country has its wrinkles, and “staying under 183” isn’t enough on its own to be off the hook.

General 183-day rule and residency: cummings.law · reference read. Your specific case: a tax advisor in the country. Not advice.

- **Double taxation:** two countries may each want to tax the same income. Double-taxation treaties exist to prevent it; knowing whether your pair of countries has one saves you real money.

- **Personal permanent establishment:** working from a country for a long stretch can create tax obligations for your company there (see Part I, section 8). That’s why some companies limit where you’re allowed to work from.

- **Don’t wing it:** moving every three months with no tax plan is the fastest way to end up owing in two countries at once.

## 7 · Your day: async, deep, with agreed overlap

**Deep work.** Long, uninterrupted blocks for the work that demands concentration. It’s where you produce what you’re paid for. In remote it’s your edge: you can protect those blocks in a way an interruption-driven office never lets you.

### Time-zone overlap

The window when you and the team are awake at the same time. You don’t need to overlap eight hours; you need to agree an overlap — sometimes just two or three hours — for the things that truly need real time, and make everything else async.

### Maker’s schedule vs manager’s schedule

The maker runs on long blocks; a single mid-morning meeting splits the day into two useless halves. The manager runs on half-hour slots. Knowing the difference helps you protect your blocks and cluster meetings instead of scattering them.

**Availability, not presence.** Put your hours and your window in writing so the team knows when to count on you. Being available in your agreed window is worth more than being “online” all day to be seen.

## 8 · Communicating well is half the job

In remote, if you don’t write it, it doesn’t count. Your ability to write clearly is as important as your craft: it’s what makes people trust you from a distance.

- **Overcommunicate your status.** Nobody sees you. A quick “here’s where I am, this is blocking me, I’ll have it Thursday” keeps you from looking vanished.

- **Write complete messages.** Context, what you need, and by when — in one message. The lone “hi” that waits for a reply before dropping the question loses hours to time zones.

- **Leave a trail for your decisions.** Make it clear why you did what you did without anyone having to ask. That’s what makes you reliable in async.

## 9 · How you’re measured and how you grow without being seen

You’re measured on what you deliver, not on the hours you show green in chat. That frees you from faking busy, but it obliges you to one thing: make your result visible.

**The remote penalty is real.** People who work remotely can be promoted more slowly than people in the boss’s sightline, even when they perform the same. It isn’t fair, but it’s measurable. You fight it by making your work undeniably visible in writing.

Kasperska et al. · PLOS ONE, 2024 · promotion penalty associated with remote work. Verify exact reference before citing.

### How to fight proximity bias from your side

- Write down what you achieve, not just what you do: outcome, not activity.

- Make your work easy to find and cite for whoever decides promotions.

- Ask for clear promotion criteria and feedback on fixed dates, not whenever.

## 10 · Your first 90 days in remote

In an office, on day one someone shows you where everything is. In remote, that someone doesn’t appear: onboarding is largely yours. How you enter shapes how you’re seen for months.

| **Stretch**    | **Your focus**                                                                   | **The signal you send**        |
|----------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Days 1–30**  | Read the handbook, learn how decisions and communication work, ask without fear. | You learn fast and on your own |
| **Days 31–60** | First real, complete deliverable. Write your status well.                        | You can be counted on          |
| **Days 61–90** | Work with autonomy. Propose, don’t just execute.                                 | You add, you don’t just comply |

**Read first, in week one.** Before you ask for a welcome call, exhaust the handbook. Asking what’s already written marks you; asking what isn’t makes you valuable.

## 11 · Your setup: gear, security, environment

- **Connection and backup:** a good one and a plan B (phone hotspot, a cafe with reliable wifi). Dropping mid-delivery isn’t an excuse that works twice.

- **Security minimum:** password manager, two-factor, and disk encryption. If you handle company data, this isn’t optional.

- **A focus environment:** a place where you can do deep work. A decent camera and mic sell more professionalism than you think in an interview or a demo.

## 12 · Digital nomad visas and mobility

**Digital nomad visa.** A permit a country grants to reside there while you work remotely for clients or companies elsewhere. It legalizes being there; it doesn’t, on its own, settle where you pay tax (see section 6).

Each country has its requirements: minimum income, health insurance, duration. Before you move, cross two things: whether you meet the visa, and what it means for you to spend more than half a year there tax-wise.
