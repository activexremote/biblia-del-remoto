# The CEO overview: running a company with no office

Building a remote company isn't taking the one you already have and sending everyone home. It's changing the mechanism your company uses to decide, produce, and trust. Skip that change and you get the worst of both worlds: the rigidity of the office and the noise of distance.

**The thesis, in one line.** A remote company done right doesn’t compete for local talent at local prices. It hires the best people on the planet, pays them for where they live, coordinates in writing, and judges by output.

## 1 · Why remote, really

There are four real levers. None of them is “save on office rent” — that’s the smallest one.

### Borderless talent access

You stop hiring within a 25-mile radius and start hiring worldwide. The bottleneck stops being who lives nearby and becomes who’s best. It’s the one lever a local competitor can’t copy.

### Geographic cost arbitrage

You pay competitively for each person’s market, not yours. The same senior profile costs wildly different amounts depending on where they get paid. You manage this with an explicit compensation policy (see section 9), not offer by offer.

### Async leverage

With people across time zones, work moves forward while you sleep. That only happens if the work is written down and doesn’t depend on two people being awake at once. Without async, international remote is a hell of 6 a.m. calls.

### Resilience

A distributed company doesn’t stop because one city has a storm, a transit strike, or an outage. There’s no single physical point of failure.

## 2 · The five levels of remote work

Matt Mullenweg’s framework tells you where a company actually stands. Almost everyone thinks they’re higher than they are. The goal isn’t “people at home”; it’s reaching level 4.

| **\#** | **What happens**                                                                                                | **The tell**                           |
|--------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **1**  | Nothing is remote. Everything happens in the office; outside it, you don’t work.                                | “Without the office we don’t function” |
| **2**  | You recreate the office online. Video calls all day, fixed hours, presence policed.                             | Zoom 9-to-6, camera required           |
| **3**  | You adapt to the medium: better tools, some writing — but the meeting still rules.                              | Good tools, office habits              |
| **4**  | Async by default. Work is written, decisions documented, you’re judged on output. The meeting is the exception. | Progress doesn’t need overlap          |
| **5**  | Nirvana. The distributed team outperforms any in-person team could.                                             | Rare, and not the daily goal           |

Matt Mullenweg · “Distributed Work’s Five Levels of Autonomy” · Automattic, 2020 · industry-standard framework · blog ma.tt and TED talk. Verify exact link before publishing.

**The level-2 trap.** Most companies stall here: they rebuild the office over webcam and conclude “remote doesn’t work.” Remote isn’t failing; the level is. Your job as founder is to push the company to 4.

## 3 · How you run it: async-first, documentation-first

**Async-first.** Your default mode of communication doesn’t wait for an instant reply: you write something complete, the other person answers when they can. Synchronous — a call, a meeting — is the exception you justify, not the norm.

Async isn’t “slower.” It’s what lets you keep the best person even if they live eight hours ahead of you, and it’s what protects everyone’s deep work.

### Documentation-first

Hard rule: if it isn’t written down, it didn’t happen. Decisions, the why behind them, and processes live in a document — not in someone’s head or a chat thread that scrolls away. You pay the cost of writing once; you pay the cost of not writing every time someone asks.

### A single source of truth

Everything that matters — how you invoice, who decides what, how you ship — has one canonical home. Two versions of the truth means zero. This bible is an example: it exists so there aren’t ten different answers to the same question.

### Handbook-first

Anything repeatable gets written once in a handbook and linked, instead of explained to each new hire. Onboarding stops being “have someone walk you through it” and becomes “read this, then ask what isn’t clear.”

## 4 · Communication: which channel for what

In remote, picking the wrong channel is the number-one source of friction. The rule is simple: the more important or delicate, the more async and written; the more urgent and ambiguous, the more synchronous.

| **What the message is**                          | **Channel**                               | **Expected reply** |
|--------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|--------------------|
| **A decision, a proposal, long context**         | Written document (proposal, decision doc) | Hours to 1–2 days  |
| **Day-to-day coordination, quick questions**     | Team chat (Slack, Teams)                  | Same day           |
| **Something tangled, tense, a misunderstanding** | Short call, with written notes after      | Scheduled          |
| **A real emergency (something’s broken)**        | On-call channel + direct mention          | Now                |

**Overcommunicate.** In an office, context leaks by osmosis: you overhear things, you read faces. Remote has no osmosis. What would be “obvious” in person has to be said in remote. Writing too much is cheap; silence is what’s expensive.

### Response times, agreed

Nobody’s obliged to reply instantly — that would kill deep work. But there is an explicit agreement — say, chat within the same business day, documents within 48 hours — so nobody stays blocked waiting and nobody lives glued to notifications.

## 5 · Meetings: the last resort, not the first

The meeting is your company’s most expensive instrument: it multiplies its length by the number of attendees and breaks everyone’s focus. In a well-run remote company, you call a meeting when writing no longer cuts it — not before.

### The written proposal replaces the status meeting

Instead of gathering ten people to “get aligned,” whoever owns a topic writes a proposal: the problem, the recommended option, the alternatives ruled out, and the decision being asked for. Others read it and respond in writing, when they can. You only call the meeting if real disagreement survives the read.

### Minimum hygiene for any meeting

- It has a document beforehand: no written agenda, no meeting.

- Only deciders and contributors attend; everyone else reads it after.

- It ends with written decisions and owners, not “a good chat.”

- It’s recorded or summarized for whoever couldn’t make it in their time zone.

**Rule of thumb.** Before you send the invite, ask: does this resolve with a document and two comments? If yes, it’s not a meeting — it’s a document.

## 6 · How work is measured: outputs, not presence

**ROWE (results-only work environment).** You’re judged on what you produce, not the hours you’re online or when. It’s the only management style that survives time zones: you can’t “watch” someone work while they sleep and you work.

### Why surveillance doesn’t work

Monitoring software — screenshots, keystrokes, screen time — measures activity, not value. What it actually achieves is teaching people to look busy and driving away your best, who are the ones who can choose where to work. You measure outputs (deliverables, goals), not inputs (hours).

### Define the result up front, not after

To judge by result, the result has to be written before the work starts: what “done” means, by when, to what quality bar. Without that up-front definition, “measuring by results” rots into measuring by vibe.

### Goals, not tasks

You hand people the problem and the success criteria, not the step list. In remote you can’t micromanage without burning out; and if you hire well, you don’t want to.

## 7 · Hiring abroad: contractor or EOR

When the person you want lives somewhere your company has no legal entity, you have two clean routes. Picking the wrong one is the remote founder’s expensive mistake.

| **Route**                    | **What it is**                                                                                                                                                   | **When it fits**                                                  |
|------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Independent contractor**   | You sign a commercial contract with a self-employed person or company. They invoice you; you’re not their employer.                                              | Project or freelance work, with real independence                 |
| **EOR (Employer of Record)** | A third party is the person’s legal employer in their country and “leases” them to you. You direct the work; the EOR handles payroll, taxes, and local benefits. | You want someone on payroll in a country where you have no entity |

**Employer of Record (EOR).** A company that already has a legal entity in the person’s country and acts as their official employer: it signs their contract, runs their payroll, withholds their taxes, and gives them the benefits local law requires. You pay the EOR a fee and keep directing the day-to-day work.

### The founder’s rule

- If the relationship looks like employment — set hours, exclusivity, embedded in the team, you supply the tools and the “how” — don’t bring them in as a contractor. Either it’s a real contractor, or it goes through an EOR.

- **The EOR cost is a per-person, per-month fee** plus local employment costs. As a market figure it tends to run in the low hundreds of dollars per person per month \[SOURCE PENDING\], but it varies a lot by provider and country.

- **Contractor gives flexibility and lower fixed cost;** EOR gives compliance and stability. Contractor flexibility is only real if the independence is real.

## 8 · The legal risks that are yours, not HR’s

Three risks hold almost all the danger. You don’t need to be a lawyer; you need to know when to call one.

### Misclassification

Treating as a contractor someone the law considers an employee. It’s the number-one risk of hiring cheap abroad. If they reclassify, you’re on the hook for back taxes, contributions, severance, and fines — in the person’s country, not yours.

**The ABC test.** The framework several jurisdictions (California among them) use to decide if someone is genuinely a contractor. They only are if all three hold: (A) they work free of your control and direction; (B) they do work outside your usual business; (C) they run their own independent trade or business. Fail one, and they’re an employee.

- **AB5 (California).** The law that hardened the ABC test. Penalties for misclassification can be severe \[SOURCE PENDING: verify current amount\].

- **IR35 (United Kingdom).** A regime that targets the “disguised employee” posing as a contractor. If the relationship is employment in substance, it’s taxed as employment.

### Permanent establishment (PE)

The risk that your people’s activity in a country makes your company “exist” there for tax purposes and owe corporate tax locally. It’s triggered, for example, when someone habitually closes contracts in your name from there. An EOR reduces this risk because the legal employer is the local provider, not you.

### Intellectual property and data

Without an IP assignment clause in the contract, what a contractor creates can stay theirs, not yours. And moving personal data across borders has its own rules (GDPR and equivalents). Both are settled in the contract, before you start — not after.

General framework, not legal advice. Tax residency and the day rule: cummings.law. Before hiring in a new country, get local legal review.

## 9 · Compensation: the choice that defines your culture

There are two philosophies, and choosing one is choosing what kind of company you are. There’s no right answer; there’s a coherent one.

| **Model**                      | **How it works**                                                                         | **The tension**                                      |
|--------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| **Geo-adjusted (geo-tiering)** | You pay to each person’s market and cost of living. Same role, different pay by country. | Fair to the market, awkward between peers            |
| **Location-agnostic**          | You pay the same for the same role, wherever the person lives.                           | Simple and motivating, pricier and harder to sustain |

**Bands by level and geography (geo-tiering).** A compensation system that sets a salary band per combination of role level and geographic zone. It gives predictability and defensibility, at the cost of two people in the same role potentially earning differently.

Whatever you decide, write it down and make it transparent internally. What sinks morale isn’t the model — it’s discovering there’s a hidden, incoherent one.

## 10 · Culture with no office: trust by default

### Proximity bias

The tendency to value more — and promote sooner — whoever is closer or more visible, even when they don’t produce more. In remote it’s lethal: it punishes people for their time zone instead of their work. There’s evidence of a real promotion penalty for people who work remotely.

Kasperska et al. · PLOS ONE, 2024 · promotion penalty associated with remote work · ~15 min read. Verify exact reference before citing.

You fight it with mechanism, not goodwill: promotion decisions based on written results, public criteria, and the boss being the first to work remotely so presence isn’t what gets rewarded.

### Trust is the default

The default model is “I assume you’re doing your job” until proven otherwise, not the reverse. Surveillance signals distrust, and distrust drives out good people. You hire adults; treat them like adults.

### Rituals that hold the bond

Async doesn’t mean cold. Deliberate moments — one in-person gathering a year, a social channel, intros for new joiners — replace the hallway coffee that remote doesn’t have. Closeness, like everything else, is designed; it doesn’t appear on its own.

## 11 · The founder’s stack

Fewer tools, used better. One source of truth per category. Duplicating tools duplicates confusion.

- **Source of truth and docs:** Notion (handbook, decisions, processes).

- **Communication:** Slack or Teams for day-to-day async; video only for what needs it.

- **Global hiring and pay:** Deel or Remote for contractors and EOR in one place.

- **Meeting notes:** Granola or similar, so what’s said gets written without effort.

## 12 · Security and devices, done distributed

With no office, every home is a perimeter and every laptop a possible leak. Security stops being “the office network” and becomes written policy and defaults.

- Password manager required, and two-factor on everything critical.

- Disk encryption on any device that touches company data.

- Access by what the person needs, not “access to everything just in case.”

- What happens to gear and access the day someone leaves: written before, not that day.
